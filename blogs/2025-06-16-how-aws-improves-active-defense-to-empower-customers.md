<p>At AWS, security is the top priority, and today we’re excited to share work we’ve been doing towards our goal to make AWS the safest place to run any workload. In earlier posts on this blog, we&nbsp;shared details of our internal active defense systems, like <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/how-aws-threat-intelligence-deters-threat-actors/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">MadPot (global honeypots)</a>, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/how-aws-tracks-the-clouds-biggest-security-threats-and-helps-shut-them-down/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Mithra (domain graph neural network)</a>, and <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/how-aws-uses-active-defense-to-help-protect-customers-from-security-threats/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Sonaris (network mitigations)</a>. We’re still&nbsp;inventing new ways to improve the effectiveness of threat intelligence and automated response to detect and help prevent attacks. Today we’ll share&nbsp;advancements in active defense related to malware, software vulnerabilities, and AWS resource misconfigurations. Like the other posts we linked to, these are constantly improving capabilities that our customers get just for being on the AWS network. We’ll discuss these topics in more depth at <a href="https://reinforce.awsevents.com/innovation-talks/?trk=registration.awsevents.com" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">re:inforce 2025 during Innovation Talk SEC302.</a></p> 
<h2>Stopping malware from spreading</h2> 
<p>Financially motivated threat actors try to gain access to a wide array of networked assets. The more resources they control, the more places they can hide, and the longer they can profit from their abusive operations. As such, threat actor malware often contains modules to scan for new targets, replicate binaries over the network, and then repeat. If left unchecked, such rapidly spreading behavior can lead to network congestion, service availability loss, and data destruction. We want to help prevent this behavior to the greatest degree possible.</p> 
<p>One effective strategy we employ is identifying the threat actor’s key infrastructure where malware is centrally controlled. We use a variety of techniques to identify, verify, track, and disrupt threat infrastructure. Using network traffic logs, honeypot interactions, and malware samples from an array of sensor positions, we mitigate botnets, abusive proxies, and peer-to-peer malware. Over the past 12 months, AWS helped prevent over 4 million malware infection attempts across 315 thousand distinct <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Amazon Elastic Compute Cloud (Amazon EC2)</a> instances. By protecting workloads from these malware infections, we not only protect our network and our customers, but also the broader internet from further malware expansion.</p> 
<p>Today, we apply active defense measures at the network routing level for customers. In some cases, we decide not to take action, because the scale of these cloud network routing changes could impact legitimate customer workloads. In these cases, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/improve-your-security-posture-using-amazon-threat-intelligence-on-aws-network-firewall/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">AWS Network Firewall now gives you control</a> over this security decision for your unique workloads. Using Amazon threat intelligence, the new <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/network-firewall/latest/developerguide/aws-managed-rule-groups-atd.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">active threat defense managed rule group</a> empowers you to protect your <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/vpc" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Amazon Virtual Private Cloud (Amazon VPC)</a> workloads against threat activities observed across AWS global infrastructure. When enabled, you can use it to configure managed rules, view indicators of compromise on matching traffic, and automatically block suspicious traffic associated with threat actor infrastructure, such as command-and-control communication, embedded URLs, and malicious domains.</p> 
<h2>Advancements in threat hunting and mitigating software vulnerabilities</h2> 
<p>At Amazon, we’re proud to support software vulnerability research with programs for <a href="https://hackerone.com/amazonvrp?type=team" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">bug bounty</a>, <a href="https://hackerone.com/aws_vdp?type=team" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">vulnerability disclosure</a>, and <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/opensource/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">open source contribution</a>. We’ve also become a more active participant in the CVE process by becoming a <a href="https://www.cve.org/PartnerInformation/ListofPartners/partner/AMZN" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">CVE Numbering Authority (CNA)</a> for the software and services provided by Amazon. Thanks to the public CVE database, we see vulnerability research accelerating as reported CVEs have grown by 21 percent year-over-year since 2013, with over 40 thousand CVEs published in 2024. This virtuous cycle of finding and resolving vulnerabilities improves cyber security over time, but AWS sees threat actors searching for unresolved vulnerabilities&nbsp;to gain unauthorized access to resources.</p> 
<p>We’ve expanded MadPot and Sonaris to identify and stop a broader range of malicious vulnerability scanning and exploitation activity, protecting every AWS customer from vulnerability exposure. We’ve added hundreds of new detections and MadPot service emulations to identify real attacks. As we’ve expanded our visibility, we’ve continued blocking hundreds of millions of CVE exploit attempts daily across the AWS network. </p> 
<p>As we’ve made these active defense systems better at stopping CVE exploit attacks, the total number of attacks has gone down by over 55 percent in the last 12 months, as shown in Figure 1. There are many factors outside our control in this observation, but we’re happy to see fewer CVE exploit attacks. This trend coincides with the detection, regionalization, latency, and guardrail improvements we’ve made in 2025. No system can block everything, so fewer exploit attempts mean less risk across a wide range of workloads.</p>

<p>This work to identify known exploits in the wild directly benefits users of <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/inspector/latest/user/findings-understanding-score.html" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">vulnerability intelligence in Amazon Inspector</a>, which provides an Amazon Inspector score for customers to prioritize where to spend security hardening resources. This includes the most recent date of observed exploitation attempts, the <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">MITRE ATT&amp;CK</a> techniques associated with the exploit activity, and the industries targeted.</p> 
<h2>Protecting architectures built on AWS</h2> 
<p>AWS actively defends compute and network resources for our customers; we also defend the distinct AWS-native resources that customers rely on. AWS access key credentials are a critical resource that allow access to customer accounts. <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/iam/resources/best-practices/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">The AWS Identity and Access Management best practices</a> share proven techniques for customers to keep their credentials from being abused. Through active defense, we do even more to help customers who haven’t yet adopted these best practices.</p> 
<p>Each day, AWS helps prevent an average of 167 million malicious scanning connections seeking unintentionally exposed AWS access key pairs. In case access keys are discovered through other means, we’ve expanded our protection of customer-managed IAM credentials. When our threat intelligence analytics show that a customer-managed credential is known by a threat actor, we put mitigations in place to restrict access to highly privileged operations. We also send customized notifications to help customers identify how the credential was exposed. These efforts are paying off for our customers every day; the following response is a good example of what we hear regularly:</p> 
<blockquote>
 <p>This is a key that we already rotated a few weeks ago based on another alarm from you. It turned out that the new rotated key happened to be in your second alarm to us. So it meant that the app that the key was linked to was still leaking it. </p> 
 <p>So on Monday we sat down with the dev team, found where the app was leaking some secrets from, we patched it, I rotated all the exposed secrets (it was more than the IAM key) and we plugged in the extra security in the app. </p> 
 <p>So thanks again for those alerts, they are very precious.<br /> – AWS Customer</p>
</blockquote> 
<p>In a specific case of threat activity in November and December of 2024, customers reported ransomware activity against their objects in <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/s3/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Amazon Simple Storage Service (Amazon S3)</a> storage. We saw that these ransom threats were highly correlated with exposed customer-managed IAM keys. We applied quarantines to the exposed keys, taking care to make sure that normal customer operations could continue safely. We re-sent our proactive notifications to customers about keys that were likely exposed, because the risk of an attack was elevated. During this period, we worked together with customers to deactivate over 30 thousand exposed credentials. Since this threat activity began, AWS has helped prevent over 943 million malicious attempts to encrypt customer Amazon S3 objects.</p> 
<p>These credential exposure detections flow into <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-amazon-guardduty-extended-threat-detection-aiml-attack-sequence-identification-for-enhanced-cloud-security/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Amazon GuardDuty Extended Threat Detection</a>, simplifying threat detection and response operations for modern cloud environments.</p> 
<h2>Better together</h2> 
<p>The approach AWS takes to active defense shows how security can be improved by layering protections across the infrastructure stack and using threat intelligence to drive risk reduction. By building active defense into our services at no extra cost, AWS helps our customers stay protected from a wide range of threats.</p> 
<p>While we continue to constantly improve our protections for our customers, some of our work is by nature probabilistic, because we never see inside customer workloads. We don’t apply active defense in situations where the detection is ambiguous, because that might impact our customers’ production systems. To stay secure, customers should never let down their own defenses. AWS security services like <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/iam" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM)</a>, <a href="https://docs.aws.amazon.com/waf/latest/developerguide/ddos-advanced-summary.html" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AWS Shield Advanced</a>, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/waf" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AWS WAF</a>, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/network-firewall/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">AWS Network Firewall</a>, <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/guardduty" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Amazon GuardDuty</a>, and <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/inspector" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Amazon Inspector</a> provide prevention, detection, and response that customers can configure for their unique needs. The good news is that by working together, we’re making the internet safer for everyone.</p>
